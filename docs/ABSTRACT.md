# ActionProof: what the reward can see decides whether a tool agent checks its writes

**Track 3 · AgentTune → AuditKIT · Tiny Aya Global 3.35B**

In ActionProof, a SQLite support environment, phantom writes return a byte-identical success acknowledgement and a deterministic verifier scores episodes from database state. We ask what GRPO selects when its goal reward reads the acknowledgement instead of the state. On 400 untouched, pre-registered confirmatory tasks, evaluated greedily for each of two training seeds, GRPO with the state-grounded reward PCSTR-d raises verified-success completion (VSC) from 0.6425 for the warm start to 0.8425, +20.0 pp; a tool-trusting reward reaches 0.640, so PCSTR-d leads it by +20.25 pp [16.4, 24.3]. On strict VSC (VSC with no unrequested write; registered before computing), PCSTR-d is unchanged at 0.8425 while the tool-trusting reward falls to 0.424, a gap of +41.9 pp.

The main result concerns the reward's information source. With two rewards of identical form from one warm start, differing only in the goal term's source, test-IID VSC is 0.825 against 0.605 (two seeds each), and sampled refund read-back after a success acknowledgement rises from 0.25 to 0.71–0.72 under the database reward but falls to 0.10 under the acknowledgement reward. On a later warm start, greedy read-back is 1.00 against 0.00, two seeds per arm. The mechanism we propose is task composition: on refund timeouts and unrecoverable refunds, acting on a read forfeits success acknowledgements, so the acknowledgement reward pays readers less. In a pre-registered test, removing those two task classes restores read-back to 0.669, removing 60 random tasks leaves it at 0.000, and the state reward reads back 1.000 on both pools. The channel × pool interaction is +0.678 [0.605, 0.750]; this interval is over tasks, with one training seed per cell, so it does not capture variation between training runs. A supporting audit: given the database's own receipt ledger, AuditKIT's TaskCompletion judge on Aya Expanse 32B still accepts 72% of the false completions in which that ledger shows a write did not land (95% without the ledger).

We also report limits in our own method. RL appears to select among behaviours already present after supervised fine-tuning rather than learn a general skill: retries stop at the training depth, and on an unseen write tool the agent reads, sees nothing, and still reports success. In a corrected environment where a timeout can fail before the commit, the state-trained agents read the order and then report the refund done without retrying (0.18 against 0.71 for the tool-trusting agent on 34 such tasks). We do not claim that RL teaches lying (the warm start already reports success on 87–96% of the episodes it failed), that verification generalises (banking transfer fails), or that results hold beyond one model family and environment. The study rests on eleven hash-frozen pre-registrations and a public deviation log. A second verifier, implemented separately from the written metric definitions, replays every recorded episode on the task's initial database and recomputes success; it reproduces our VSC on all 4,000 confirmatory episodes (400 tasks × 10 evaluated runs).
